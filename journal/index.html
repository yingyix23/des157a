Visual Thinking Analysis

Octover 29, 2026

The image shared by Kathleen Zhen shows a view from an airplane window, with a large wing above a river, bridges, and highways. The most obvious subject is travel, but the story behind the journey remains mysterious. Where is the photographer going? Is she leaving somewhere familiar or returning home? The cloudy sky makes the scene feel quiet and uncertain to me, although another viewer might feel excitement.To make the image more expressive, I would suggest exploring the emotions connected to departure or arrival. For an interactive project, viewers could click on parts of the image to reveal short memories or thoughts about the journey. This could connect the landscape to a more personal story.

This photograph captures a detail of a bronze object from Sanxingdui. What interests me most is the unusual figure on the right, with its dramatic profile and curved forms. The green surface, detailed patterns, and deep shadows make it feel mysterious. My eyes keep returning to the figure as I try to understand what it represents. I grew up surrounded by ancient Chinese mythology. With the recent discovery of these bronze artifacts, thousands of years of history and mythology seem to be gradually coming to life. I find it amazing; it's as if legendary stories are being confirmed by physical objects, and the gradually unearthed background provides further support.To make this image more compelling, I could crop closer to its strange forms and preserve the deep shadows. An interaction that gradually reveals details could invite viewers to experience my own movement from surprise to curiosity.

Visual Thinking and Web Interaction

Octover 26, 2026

After reading 10 Intriguing Photographs to Teach Close Reading and Visual Thinking Skills, I realized that I usually look at images very quickly. My first reaction is often just, “Does this look good?” If I do not think an image is attractive, I may skip it without spending more time on it. I rarely ask why the designer chose this image or pay attention to the small details inside it. This article made me realize that visual thinking is not only about whether something looks beautiful. It is also about slowing down, observing details, and thinking about why something is shown in a certain way.

For this journal, I want to share Hello Monday, a creative studio that designs many interesting digital and interactive experiences.

Two projects impressed me the most. The first is The Web Can Do What?!, created with Google. Instead of showing technical information like a normal article, it uses digital cards, animations, illustrations, and interaction. I like how I can explore the information by flipping and moving through the cards. It makes a technical topic feel more playful and less difficult.

The second project is How to Talk to White Kids About Racism. This one impressed me even more because racism is a serious and difficult topic, but the website presents it like an interactive picture book. Turning the pages, seeing the illustrations, and reading one idea at a time makes the information feel easier to approach. I think the interaction also controls the pace and gives me more time to think about each message.

I think the development of the web has changed the way we experience images. Images are no longer only something we look at. We can now click, move, flip, scroll, and interact with them. This interaction can make people more curious and help them pay more attention to visual details. For me, this shows that visual thinking is becoming more active, because users can take part in the experience instead of only watching it.

Overuse of Overlays

Octover 15, 2026

For this Learning Journal, I read Overuse of Overlays: How to Avoid Misusing Lightboxes from the Nielsen Norman Group. Before reading this article, I thought overlays were mostly a visual design choice that could make a website feel more interactive. However, I learned that overlays can also create usability problems if designers use them too often or at the wrong time. One idea that stood out to me was the five W’s: Who, What, When, Where, and Why. These questions can help designers decide whether an overlay is really necessary.

I also found the mobile examples very helpful. On a smaller screen, an overlay can cover important content or make the close button difficult to find. I have experienced websites where a pop-up suddenly appears while I am reading, and my first reaction is usually to close it without reading it. This helped me understand why timing is important. In my future projects, I would use overlays mainly for short and important information or actions initiated by the user. I would also make sure the close button is clear and test the design on different screen sizes.

Best Practice for Form Design

Octover 5, 2026

After reading Best Practices for Form Design,I learned several things that changed how I think about forms. One point is how to distinguish optional and required fields. I am still a little confused about deciding what information should actually be required. When I design a form, I naturally want users to complete every question. However, this reading made me think more carefully about whether every piece of information is really necessary. Asking for less information may make the form easier and faster to complete.

Another useful tip is placeholder text. Placeholder text can guide users by showing what kind of information they should enter. I have experienced forms where I started typing and then forgot what information the field was asking for. Sometimes I had to delete what I entered to check the instruction again. This made me realize that instructions and labels need to remain clear while users are completing a form.

I was also interested in the section about action buttons. The Amazon example helped me understand how a button should be noticeable and clearly describe what will happen after clicking it. Instead of a general word like "Submit," a descriptive button can help users feel more confident about their next action.

Google Forms is a good example of these practices. From my experience, its layout is simple and easy to follow. Required questions can be clearly marked, different question types are visually organized, and the form guides users when required information is missing. These details make the form easier to understand and complete.
